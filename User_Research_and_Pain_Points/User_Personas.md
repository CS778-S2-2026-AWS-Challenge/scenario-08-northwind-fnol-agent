# Insurance FNOL User Personas

## Purpose

This document defines three evidence-informed user personas for the First Notice of Loss (FNOL) experience. The personas were developed from:

- an exploratory user survey;
- analysis of existing FNOL journeys and common claim fields;
- public customer reviews and complaints;
- research into current digital claim channels and convenience features;
- research into claim verification and fraud-control practices.

The personas focus on users' behaviours, goals, needs, and claim situations. Demographic details such as age, occupation, and income are intentionally excluded because the available evidence does not support them.

## Segmentation Logic

The first two personas are segmented by previous claim experience:

- **64.3%** had never made an insurance claim;
- **35.7%** had previously made an insurance claim.

The third persona is segmented by **claim situation and support needs**, rather than previous experience. A first-time or experienced claimant may both enter this state when a claim becomes urgent, complex, unusual, or disputed.

| Experience dimension | Routine or straightforward situation | Urgent, complex, or disputed situation |
|---|---|---|
| No previous claim experience | Persona 1: First-Time Claimant | Persona 3 support mode |
| Previous claim experience | Persona 2: Efficiency-Seeking Claimant | Persona 3 support mode |

## Survey Overview

### Claim experience and process confidence

| Measure | Survey result |
|---|---:|
| Never made an insurance claim | 64.3% |
| Previously made an insurance claim | 35.7% |
| Moderate understanding of policies and the claim process (3/5) | 57.1% |
| Higher understanding (4/5) | 21.4% |
| Lower understanding (1–2/5) | 21.4% |
| Complete understanding (5/5) | 0% |

No respondent considered themselves fully confident in understanding insurance policies and the claim process. Previous claim experience therefore does not necessarily mean that a user fully understands coverage, evidence requirements, or the insurer's internal workflow.

### Shared priorities

| Priority when reporting a claim | Survey result |
|---|---:|
| Knowing exactly what information is required | 64.3% |
| Understanding whether the incident may be covered | 50.0% |
| Being able to provide photos or documents | 50.0% |
| Knowing what happens next | 42.9% |
| Being able to track claim progress | 42.9% |
| Completing the report quickly | 35.7% |

Channel preferences were distributed across mobile apps and chat or messaging (50.0% each), as well as websites, phone calls, and in-person service (42.9% each). This suggests that users expect channel choice and continuity rather than a single mandatory reporting channel.

### Attitudes towards AI assistance

When human support could be requested at any time, comfort with using an AI assistant averaged approximately **3.07 out of 5**.

| Comfort rating | Survey result |
|:---:|---:|
| 1 | 14.3% |
| 2 | 0% |
| 3 | 57.1% |
| 4 | 21.4% |
| 5 | 7.1% |

The strongest concerns were:

| Concern about using AI during a claim | Survey result |
|---|---:|
| AI misunderstanding what happened | 78.6% |
| AI struggling with a complex or unusual situation | 64.3% |
| AI making decisions that should be made by a human | 57.1% |
| Privacy or security of personal information | 42.9% |
| Not knowing when a human would become involved | 21.4% |

The survey therefore supports AI as an assistant for guidance, information collection, structuring, and routing—not as the sole decision-maker for complex or high-impact claims.

## Persona 1: The First-Time Claimant

### Profile

| Attribute | Description |
|---|---|
| **Persona name** | Nina — an illustrative name, not a demographic claim |
| **Survey segment** | 64.3% had never made an insurance claim |
| **Typical situation** | A minor vehicle accident or another insured event has just occurred, and the user needs to report it for the first time |
| **Process familiarity** | Low; unfamiliar with the correct sequence, insurance terminology, and evidence requirements |
| **Emotional state** | Anxious, uncertain, and concerned about making a mistake |
| **Primary goal** | Understand what to do immediately and complete the first report correctly |
| **Main barrier** | Not knowing which actions, information, and evidence are required |
| **Preferred support** | Step-by-step guidance, plain language, contextual questions, confirmation, and accessible human help |

### Persona statement

> “I have never made a claim before. Before showing me a long form, tell me what I need to do now and what evidence I must keep.”

This is a synthesised persona statement rather than a verbatim survey response.

### Supporting survey evidence

When people with no previous claim experience imagined being involved in a minor vehicle accident, they most wanted to know:

| Information needed | Survey result |
|---|---:|
| What to do immediately after the incident | 77.8% |
| What photos or evidence to collect | 55.6% |
| Whether the police should be contacted | 44.4% |
| Whether the insurance may cover the damage | 44.4% |
| How long the process may take | 33.3% |
| Already knew what to do | 22.2% |

The first-time claimant's primary need is therefore not simply a shorter form. It is **situational guidance at the moment of uncertainty**.

### Behaviours

- Looks for immediate instructions before attempting a complete claim form.
- Worries about missing evidence that may be impossible to collect later.
- May not know whether police, towing, or emergency support is required.
- Does not naturally organise the incident into insurer-defined data fields.
- May switch between website, app, chat, phone, or in-person support to find reassurance.
- Relies on explicit confirmation that each important step has been completed.

### Jobs to Be Done

**Functional job**

> When an insured incident happens for the first time, I want the service to explain what I should do and ask only the questions relevant to my situation, so I do not miss an important step because I lack experience.

**Emotional job**

> I want reassurance that I have not made a mistake and that my lack of claim experience will not disadvantage me.

### Pain points

1. Does not know whether to focus first on safety, evidence, police contact, or reporting the claim.
2. Does not know which photos, documents, or third-party details will be required.
3. Finds insurance terminology difficult and cannot confidently interpret coverage.
4. Cannot distinguish relevant questions from fields intended for other claim types.
5. Remains uncertain after submission about whether enough information was provided.
6. Worries that an AI assistant may misunderstand an unstructured description.

### Needs

- Immediate safety and incident guidance before the full report begins.
- A clear distinction between information required now and information that can be added later.
- Contextual prompts for photos, third-party details, police references, and loss mitigation.
- Plain-language explanations of insurance terms.
- Natural-language reporting followed by a structured summary for user confirmation.
- Clear acknowledgement, a claim number, missing-information status, and next steps.
- Human support available throughout the journey.

### Ideal experience

1. The service first checks for injuries, danger, or urgent support needs.
2. The user receives a short, situation-specific action checklist.
3. The user describes the incident in natural language.
4. The system extracts claim information and asks only relevant follow-up questions.
5. The user reviews and corrects a structured summary.
6. The service confirms submission and shows the claim number, missing evidence, ownership, and next step.

### Success criteria

- The user can clearly explain what they need to do next.
- The user knows which evidence must be collected immediately.
- The FNOL can be completed without prior knowledge of the full claim process.
- The user can verify how their description was interpreted before submission.
- Submission status, missing information, and the next action are visible.

## Persona 2: The Efficiency-Seeking Claimant

### Profile

| Attribute | Description |
|---|---|
| **Persona name** | Ethan — an illustrative name, not a demographic claim |
| **Survey segment** | 35.7% had previously made an insurance claim |
| **Typical situation** | The user understands the basic idea of making a claim but does not want to repeat a slow, fragmented process |
| **Process familiarity** | Moderate; familiar with reporting and evidence, but not with every policy or internal workflow |
| **Emotional state** | Impatient with unnecessary effort and concerned about repeated follow-up |
| **Primary goal** | Complete the initial report in one attempt and receive proactive progress updates |
| **Main barrier** | Too many questions, repeated information, fragmented document requests, and unclear status |
| **Preferred support** | Pre-filled information, dynamic questions, a consolidated evidence checklist, and proactive updates |

### Persona statement

> “I understand the basic process, but do not make me answer irrelevant questions or provide the same information again a few days later.”

### Previous claim behaviour

The most recent claims in this segment covered motor vehicles, personal belongings, home or property, travel, and health. Multiple claim types could be selected, so the percentages are not intended to total 100%.

The initial reporting channels were:

| Channel | Survey result |
|---|---:|
| Website or online form | 40% |
| Phone call | 20% |
| Mobile app | 20% |
| Email | 20% |

Experienced claimants also move across multiple channels. Their need is not only a better form but continuity across the entire claim journey.

### Supporting survey evidence: immediately after the incident

| Difficulty or uncertainty | Survey result |
|---|---:|
| Did not know which photos or evidence were required | 80% |
| Did not know what to do first | 60% |
| Did not know which information to collect | 60% |
| Was unsure whether the incident was covered | 60% |
| Felt stressed and found the situation difficult to manage | 60% |
| Was unsure whether police involvement was required | 40% |

Previous claim experience did not eliminate uncertainty. A new claim type or situation may still make an experienced user behave like a first-time claimant.

### Supporting survey evidence: while reporting

| Difficulty or frustration | Survey result |
|---|---:|
| Insurance terminology was difficult | 60% |
| The process took too long | 40% |
| There were too many questions | 40% |
| Previously provided information had to be repeated | 40% |
| Uploading photos or documents was difficult | 40% |
| It was unclear what information was required | 20% |
| Customer service involved waiting | 20% |

### Supporting survey evidence: after submission

| Difficulty or uncertainty | Survey result |
|---|---:|
| Did not know how long the process would take | 80% |
| Was unsure whether enough information had been provided | 60% |
| Did not know whom to contact if there was a problem | 60% |
| Had to contact the insurer for updates | 60% |
| Did not know what would happen next | 40% |
| Did not know the current claim status | 40% |

Additional information or documents were requested once in 20% of cases and multiple times in 20%. No further information was requested in 40%, while 20% could not remember.

When asked to identify the single most difficult part of the experience:

- 40% selected knowing what information or evidence to collect;
- 40% selected obtaining claim updates;
- 20% selected understanding insurance coverage.

### Jobs to Be Done

**Functional job**

> When I make another claim, I want the service to reuse existing customer and policy information, ask only questions relevant to the incident, and proactively communicate progress, so I can complete the report with minimal rework.

**Emotional job**

> I want to feel that my time is respected and that I do not need to chase the insurer to move the claim forward.

### Pain points

1. Each claim feels as if it starts from zero despite an existing customer and policy relationship.
2. Static forms contain too many questions that may not apply to the current incident.
3. Evidence requirements are fragmented and may only become clear after submission.
4. Different channels or staff members ask for the same information again.
5. The user cannot see the expected timeline, current owner, or next action.
6. Updates often depend on the user contacting the insurer.
7. Insurance terminology and decision explanations remain difficult to understand.

### Needs

- Pre-filled customer, policy, and contact information.
- Dynamic questioning based on claim type and previous answers.
- A completeness check and consolidated evidence checklist before submission.
- Reliable photo and document upload with visible receipt status.
- Conversation transcripts, structured fields, attachments, and requests linked to the same claim.
- Proactive status, responsibility, timeline, and delay updates.
- No repetition of information already received and confirmed.
- Plain-language explanations tied to specific facts, evidence, and policy requirements.

### Open-response themes

The improvements requested in open responses focused on:

- proactive progress reporting;
- shorter claim turnaround time;
- fewer document requests;
- completing the required work in one interaction;
- greater overall efficiency.

These themes show that efficiency is not limited to form-completion speed. It includes reducing rework, waiting, and unnecessary contact throughout the claim journey.

### Ideal experience

1. The service recognises the customer and policy after login.
2. The user selects or describes the incident.
3. Irrelevant questions are skipped automatically.
4. A consolidated list shows all currently missing information and evidence.
5. The user reviews the structured summary and submits the FNOL.
6. Status, responsibility, next action, and expected timing are updated proactively.
7. Any human or specialist continues from the existing claim context.

### Success criteria

- The number of questions changes appropriately with claim type and context.
- Repeated questions and duplicate document requests are reduced.
- Users understand whether the initial report is complete before submission.
- Key updates are delivered without requiring the user to chase the insurer.
- The next action, current owner, and expected timing remain visible.

## Persona 3: The Urgent or Complex Claimant

### Profile

| Attribute | Description |
|---|---|
| **Persona name** | Sarah — an illustrative name, not a demographic claim |
| **Segmentation basis** | A situational persona that may overlap with either experience segment |
| **Typical situation** | A serious accident, injury, uninhabitable property, multi-party event, evidence conflict, coverage dispute, or situation the digital process cannot handle |
| **Process familiarity** | Variable; claim complexity may exceed self-service capability regardless of experience |
| **Emotional state** | Highly stressed, vulnerable, and concerned about safety or significant financial impact |
| **Primary goal** | Reach an authorised human who receives the full context and can take responsibility |
| **Main barrier** | Automated journeys struggle with exceptions, and handoffs may require the user to repeat everything |
| **Preferred support** | Early risk detection, clear escalation, human decision-making, complete handoff context, and visible ownership |

### Persona statement

> “This is not a routine situation that a form can resolve. Connect me to someone who can handle it, and do not make me explain everything again.”

### Supporting survey evidence: when human support is preferred

| Situation | Survey result |
|---|---:|
| Immediately after a serious or stressful incident | 64.3% |
| The situation is complex or unusual | 50.0% |
| The digital process is difficult to use | 50.0% |
| There is a disagreement about the claim | 50.0% |
| The required information is not understood | 42.9% |
| Usually prefers to complete the process entirely without human support | 7.1% |

Human support should therefore be a visible, expected part of the FNOL design—not a hidden fallback available only after automation fails.

### Situational characteristics

This persona is defined by one or more of the following characteristics:

- **Urgency:** injury, towing, emergency repairs, temporary accommodation, or continuing loss.
- **Complexity:** multiple vehicles, third parties, witnesses, police involvement, or multiple insurers.
- **Exception:** the incident does not fit available form options or automated rules.
- **Dispute:** liability, coverage, exclusion, evidence, valuation, fraud, or non-disclosure concerns.
- **Vulnerability:** high stress, language or accessibility needs, or inability to complete a digital process.
- **High impact:** misunderstanding or delay could create significant safety, financial, or legal consequences.

### Jobs to Be Done

**Functional job**

> When my claim is urgent, complex, or disputed, I want the system to recognise the risk and transfer me to the correct human team with my conversation, information, evidence, and escalation reason, so the claim can continue without restarting.

**Emotional job**

> I want confidence that a responsible person is making high-impact decisions and that automation will not trap me when I most need help.

### Pain points

1. Routine digital questions do not reflect the seriousness or complexity of the incident.
2. AI may misunderstand the event and continue using incorrect structured information.
3. The user cannot see when a human will become involved or how to request one.
4. Automation may cross into decisions that require human judgement.
5. The conversation, claim fields, evidence, and escalation reason may not reach the human agent.
6. Responsibility becomes fragmented across staff, assessors, repairers, and specialists.
7. Verification or dispute decisions lack specific explanations and correction paths.
8. Repeating a distressing event increases the user's emotional burden.

### Needs

- Early questions about safety, injury, continuing loss, and urgent support.
- A visible human-support option at every stage.
- Escalation based on severity, complexity, dispute, exception, and vulnerability signals.
- Correct routing to a claims team, broker, assessor, repairer, or specialist.
- Full transfer of the transcript, confirmed fields, attachments, missing items, risk indicators, and escalation reason.
- Clear communication about who has taken ownership and expected response time.
- Human oversight for liability, coverage, fraud, and final claim decisions.
- Information correction, explanation, review, and complaint pathways.

### Appropriate AI boundary

| AI can support | Human responsibility or oversight is required |
|---|---|
| Collect a natural-language incident description | Make substantive decisions in serious or complex claims |
| Extract and display structured FNOL fields | Determine liability, coverage, fraud, or claim denial |
| Identify missing information and ask relevant questions | Resolve conflicting evidence and disputes |
| Provide approved safety and evidence guidance | Support vulnerable users and high-impact communication |
| Produce a user-confirmed claim summary | Explain decisions that materially affect user rights |
| Detect situations that require escalation | Accept responsibility and determine the next course of action |

### Ideal experience

1. The service detects urgency, complexity, vulnerability, or dispute signals early.
2. Immediate safety and loss-mitigation guidance is provided.
3. The user is told which team will receive the case and the expected wait.
4. The transcript, structured fields, evidence, and escalation reason are transferred together.
5. The human continues from the existing record rather than restarting the interview.
6. Ownership and proactive communication remain clear throughout the claim.

### Success criteria

- Serious and complex signals are identified early.
- Users can request a human at any stage.
- Confirmed information is not recollected after handoff.
- Users know who owns the next action and why escalation occurred.
- High-impact decisions include human oversight, specific explanations, and a review path.

## Persona Comparison

| Dimension | First-Time Claimant | Efficiency-Seeking Claimant | Urgent or Complex Claimant |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Segmentation basis** | No previous claim experience | Previous claim experience | Current claim context and support intensity |
| **Core problem** | Does not know how to begin | Understands the basics but finds the journey inefficient | Routine self-service cannot safely handle the situation |
| **Most important first step** | Tell me what to do now | Reuse what is already known and reduce repetition | Secure safety and connect the correct human team |
| **Primary information need** | Action order, evidence, police, and coverage guidance | Consolidated requirements, completeness, and progress | Risk, exception, dispute, and handoff information |
| **Primary emotion** | Uncertainty and fear of making a mistake | Frustration with rework and waiting | High stress and need for trust |
| **Expected role of AI** | Guide and explain | Pre-fill, simplify, structure, and update | Detect limits, escalate, and preserve context |
| **Main AI risk** | Misunderstanding the first description | Repeating questions or generating unnecessary work | Mishandling complexity or making unauthorised decisions |
| **Ideal outcome** | Successfully completes FNOL without prior knowledge | Completes the report with minimal rework and receives proactive updates | A capable human takes over immediately with full context |

## Design Principles Derived from the Personas

1. **Guide first, form second.** Understand the situation and explain the immediate action before presenting the full information request.
2. **Ask only what is relevant.** Questions should respond to claim type, incident details, and previous answers.
3. **Tell us once.** Conversations, structured fields, attachments, and document requests should remain connected.
4. **Confirm AI understanding.** Users should review and correct a structured claim summary before submission.
5. **Show what happens next.** Display the claim number, current status, owner, missing items, next action, and expected timing.
6. **Communicate proactively.** Notify users about progress, delays, and information requests without requiring repeated follow-up.
7. **Design human support into the journey.** Human assistance is a formal path for urgent, complex, disputed, and vulnerable situations.
8. **Use AI to assist; keep humans accountable.** AI can collect and structure information, while high-impact decisions retain human oversight.
9. **Explain verification.** State why evidence or historical information is required and do not treat normal discrepancies as automatic fraud.
10. **Maintain cross-channel context.** Website, app, chat, phone, in-person, and specialist channels should continue the same claim journey.

## Research Limitations

- The survey sample was small and exploratory, so the percentages should not be treated as population estimates.
- The previous-claim branch was especially limited; its percentages support preliminary persona formation but not industry-wide prevalence claims.
- Some questions allowed multiple selections, so their percentages are not expected to total 100%.
- The urgent or complex persona is situational and should not be added to the experience-segment percentages.
- Survey answers describe self-reported or hypothetical experiences rather than direct observation of insurer operations.
- AI acceptance was measured on the condition that human support could be requested at any time; it does not indicate support for fully automated claim decisions.
- Further validation should include semi-structured interviews, usability testing, and real competitor FNOL journey testing.

## Summary

> **First-time claimants need to know how to begin. Experienced claimants need to complete the report with less rework and receive proactive updates. Urgent or complex claimants need the right human to take over immediately with the full context.**

Together, the personas support a hybrid FNOL direction: AI assists with guidance, extraction, follow-up questions, structuring, and routing; users confirm critical information; and humans retain responsibility for urgent, complex, disputed, and high-impact decisions.
